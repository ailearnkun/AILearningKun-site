Not every operational task suits automation. Forcing automation onto the wrong things adds work rather than removing it.

These are the boundaries I use to sort them.

## What suits automation

**Repetitive work with clear rules.**
Record-keeping, rescheduling, reminders, assembling routine reports. If the rules can be written down, they can usually be automated.

**Data collection and consolidation.**
Pulling numbers from several sources and combining them. Tedious for humans, easy for systems.

**Continuous monitoring.**
Things that need constant checking: availability, deadlines, small deviations. People tire; systems don't.

**Delivering information to channels already in use.**
Sending summaries where the team already works, rather than forcing a migration.

## What should stay manual

**Decisions involving nuance.**
Judging whether a complaining guest warrants compensation, or whether damage was negligence or wear. The context is too rich for rigid rules.

**Relationships with people.**
Negotiation, sensitive complaint handling, building trust. Automation here actively damages.

**Rare and unpredictable situations.**
If something happens once a year, building automation isn't worth it. Write a clear manual procedure instead.

**Things whose standard isn't stable yet.**
If the process still changes monthly, automation locks in the wrong way. Stabilize first.

## How to sort quickly

Ask three questions of every task:

1. **How many times a month?** If fewer than twice, it's probably not worth it.
2. **Can the rules be written without many exceptions?** If exceptions outnumber rules, hold off.
3. **Is a mistake expensive?** If so, the automation needs checks, not just execution.

## Common mistakes

**Automating a mess.** If the process is messy, automation just makes the mess run faster. Clean it up first, then automate.

**Automating without measurement.** Without a baseline, nobody knows whether the automation genuinely helps or just moves work around.

**Automating then abandoning.** An uncared-for system slowly drifts wrong, and people revert to the old way. Better not to automate than to automate and neglect.
